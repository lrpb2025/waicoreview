As AI governance debates move forward, an instructive parallel can be found in the UN climate process. At its 15th meeting in Bangkok, the Katowice Committee of Experts on the Impacts of the Implementation of Response Measures (KCI) is scheduled to assess how countries assess the impacts of their climate policies, and on building the capacity of developing countries to do this themselves. Behind those assessments sits a data challenge that echoes the concerns of this review. Governments report limited access to disaggregated data, limited modelling capacity and heavy reliance on external consultants. AI tools may help fill some data gaps, but they raise familiar questions about data integrity, transparency and accountability: how do we know which values are measured and which are estimated, and who checks them? We suggest three principles. Locally collected data should be the foundation, with AI as a documented, human-reviewed complement. Estimated data should be clearly labelled so that oversight is possible. And building national data collection capacity should be treated as a just transition opportunity, creating skilled local employment while narrowing the digital divide.
